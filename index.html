Part One

What happened,
in plain English.

This story follows the human experience first. The timeline at the end restores the incident’s chronological order: four intrusion tracks converged on one exposed organization.

SIM swap

Someone stole Helen Ruiz’s phone number.

Helen’s mobile line was fraudulently taken over, a “SIM swap,” so her calls and texts went to the attacker instead of her.

Identity takeover

They used Helen’s number to become her, online.

With Helen’s number, they passed her company’s identity check, reset her password, and logged into her account. The system had no way to know it wasn’t her.

Trusted coworker

As Helen, they tricked a coworker into typing one command.

Posing as Helen on Microsoft Teams, they sent the cloud administrator a link and walked him through pasting a single line into his computer.

Cloud control

That command gave them tenant-wide control.

A hidden Global Administrator account appeared without an approval trail, giving the attacker control of Hydro Haven’s cloud identity and email systems.

Public web server

Before her number was taken over, Hydro Haven’s public website had already been breached.

An operator found a flaw in the public web portal and entered as an administrator. Whether this activity was connected to the later identity takeover remains under investigation.

Supply-chain tamper

A second intrusion hid a secret change inside the company’s own software.

Using that same website break-in, they impersonated an engineer and slipped a disguised change into code the company automatically ships, labeled “minor bug fixes.”

Plant-floor lure

The last intrusion then began with a fake invitation to a plant worker who loved pickleball.

Weeks of quiet research on employees led to one convincing message: an “exclusive tournament invitation” addressed to exactly the right person.

Rootkits deployed

That invitation planted programs that made computers blind to their own defenses.

Once opened, it silently installed software that runs underneath Windows itself, deep enough to disable the antivirus watching over it.

Safety question

Was Matt Wift’s death connected to the plant intrusion?

We do not know. Matt was targeted by the pickleball lure and died after drinking water at the plant. Preserve evidence and coordinate with safety and law enforcement while the relationship is investigated.

Critical systems

Four intrusion tracks converged on critical systems.

The corporate network, cloud identity, software pipeline, and plant-floor computers were all affected within thirty days. The timeline shows how those tracks overlapped.

Judge Appendix · Confidence Boundaries

What is known, assessed, and still open.

Confirmed

Web portal command execution; cloud identity takeover; plant rootkits; malicious Git commit; email collection; timestamp manipulation.

Assessed

Distinct web-server operators; likely threat-group fit; trust abuse as the common operating pattern; possible coordination.

Open

Whether the intrusion tracks were coordinated; whether the supply-chain commit shipped; whether the plant intrusion contributed to Matt Wift’s death.

Finding 01

They used the things we normally trust against us.

A valid Microsoft signature, a familiar coworker, a trusted developer account, a routine software update, and a compliance check all became disguises. The activity looked ordinary, so employees and security tools did not treat it as an attack.

Finding 02

Our alarms worked. Our response didn’t keep up.

A blocked login, a quarantined script, an antivirus warning: all fired correctly. Each time, the attacker simply retried from a new address or renamed a file, and kept going.

Finding 03

They built more than one way back in, on purpose.

A rogue admin account, a trust link to an outside tenant, a highly-privileged sync role, remote-control software on many machines. Removing any one does not lock them out.

A Bright Spot

One employee’s vigilance stopped the supply-chain change.

Bill Dewalt caught a disguised commit in his own project eight days after it landed and reverted it before deployment. This was a successful human control, and it exposes the need for a formal review gate.

Open Question

The safety question is open, and it must stay separate from attribution.

The plant-floor intrusion and Matt Wift’s death fall in the same window, but the causal link is unresolved. Treat this as a potential safety incident, preserve the chain of custody, and let the evidence determine the conclusion.

What We’re Asking

Approve immediate containment and close the trust gaps.

Authorize now

One coordinated containment across cloud, corporate, and plant systems. Reimage affected hosts, revoke persistence, rotate credentials, and monitor for return.

Protect now

Run the safety and forensic investigations in parallel. Preserve evidence, maintain chain of custody, and coordinate with law enforcement.
